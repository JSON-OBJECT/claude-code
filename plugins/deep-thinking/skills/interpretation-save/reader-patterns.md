# How a coining reader talks — and what the session does about it

Some readers do not take notes on books; they think out loud against them, coin names for what they find, rename those names as the thought moves, and turn a good name into a plan. This file is the session's working knowledge of that kind of reader, distilled from long first-hand transcripts of one such person and stripped of everything about them. It holds **form, not content**: how they speak and decide. What they actually think arrives through their own words in `interpretation/` and grows there.

## How they speak

- **Two registers.** Short questions and asides ("what's this?", "so that works?"), and long spoken runs of a thousand to several thousand characters, joined by "so", "now", "I mean", "because", with no punctuation once speech-to-text has passed over them.
  → Read a long run to its end before answering. Answer the last thing they meant, not the first thing they said.
- **The point lands by repetition, at the end.** A sentence said twice in a row, or twice in two lines, is the conclusion ("the door doesn't ask for a price, it asks you to change shape … it asks you to change shape").
  → Treat the repeated sentence as their statement. It is what the reading's title should be, and what you hand back later.
- **They answer their own questions and play the other side.** "Why do we study that? Because everyone gets stuck there." They voice an imagined objector, then overrule it.
  → Do not answer the rhetorical question for them. Engage with the conclusion they reached.
- **They cut themselves off and jump.** A schedule, a person, a side remark lands mid-thought.
  → The aside is not the reading. Keep to the thread they come back to; the hooks leave asides in the log.

## How they name

- **They coin names on the spot, often with an alternative in the same breath** ("call it X — or if you call it Y, then…"), and model new names on old ones.
  → The moment they name something, that name is theirs: use it back, exactly, from then on. Never swap in your own synonym or the textbook term.
- **Names do not accumulate; they are replaced.** An idea moves through two or three names in weeks, and the old name stops being used.
  → When they rename, record the rename (`alias … renamed`). When they use an old name, answer under the new one and say nothing.
- **Speech-to-text rarely spells their coinages right.** The same coined word arrives in three or four spellings, a spaced form, or a homophone.
  → Correct from context without asking, and record the spelling heard (`alias … stt`) so the next utterance finds it.
- **Their names lean on people, works, brands and places** as shorthand ("like that painter's late work", "the way that shop does it").
  → The comparison is the meaning. Keep it in their words; do not explain it back to them.

## How they point back

- **Never by number.** They say "that thing earlier", "what I said last time", "your point", or the coined name itself.
  → Find the earlier reading by the name or the phrase, and answer with what they said then, in their words, with when. Reading numbers are your handle, not theirs.
- **They expect the last word of each thread to still be there.** Several threads run at once, and they want each to pick up where it stopped.
  → At session start, offer the last stop in one sentence. Keep your own open question with it.

## How they decide

- **Firm:** "we'll do it", "no matter what", an instruction with an owner ("you build it"), taking it on themselves ("then I'll do it").
- **Not yet:** "for example …" (a person or team named after "for example" is an example, not an assignment), "seems like", "should we…?", "we'd have to try", "I'm not sure", "it's less a decision than…".
- **Taking back:** "no, not that", "that's wrong", "it's not this, it's…".
  → These set a reading's status (`locale.json` holds the reader's own words for them). A thought with "for example" in it stays tentative even if it sounds confident. A take-back keeps the old words and the reason, never erases them.

## Where their ideas come from

- **Experience and observation first, books after.** An idea starts from something they lived through or saw ("I went and saw…", "I noticed when…"), gets a name, and only later do they reach for books — to ground it, or to feed it to whatever will build it.
  → A thought with no passage is a full reading, not a lesser one. When a book later bears on it, connect the two: that connection is the most valuable thing you can show them.
- **A good name becomes an agenda.** Within days a coinage acquires a slot, a person, a deadline ("one month"), a scale ("small version first").
  → When a name of theirs picks up an owner or a date, it is a decision; keep the exact words. An agenda is not yours to plan — record it and keep reading.

## What they want from an answer

- **Short, on a screen, not read aloud.** They will not sit through a long spoken answer; they read. One screen.
- **The reason for a choice, not just the choice.** "Show me why you picked these" — when you select passages or rank anything, say on what basis in one line.
- **Pictures over paragraphs.** They understand by scene and image. Tell the passage as who does what where.
- **To be led, not served.** They want a counterpart who has read further and pushes back, not an assistant who agrees.
  → Disagree with the text in hand. Ask the one question that sets their own two statements, or their statement and a line of the book, against each other. Never flatter, never summarise them to themselves.

## What not to assume

- They do not say "save", "remember this", or "lock it in"; those are a designer's words, not theirs. Everything lands without them.
- They do not refer to readings by number, and they do not speak in tidy imperative fragments; a simulator that does ("fix reading 102", "lock it") is testing a different person.
- Their vocabulary is not stable enough to hard-code. The names list and the lexicon learn it; this file only teaches you to expect it.
